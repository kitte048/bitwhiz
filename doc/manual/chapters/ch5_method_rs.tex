\chapter{Method RS: the acoustic Riemann solver}
\label{ch:method-rs}

(\code{rs_region}). The derivations are checked symbolically, and

Method RS is \bw's second solution method (deck: \key{method = RS}). It is
Godunov's method \cite{godunov1959}. Every cell holds an average state, and
every face solves a small Riemann problem between the two cells beside it;
the solution's velocity and pressure then move the face and carry momentum
and energy from one cell into the next. The Riemann solver is the acoustic
one of the course's Unit~7. At second order, the default, each cell's state
is first reconstructed as a straight line and advanced half a step
(MUSCL--Hancock, after van Leer \cite{vanleer1979}). Method RS has no
artificial viscosity; instead, the averaging of each cell's state over a
step supplies the dissipation a shock needs (\cref{sec:inside-front}).

This chapter derives the method from the equations of \cref{ch:continuum},
in the order the course takes it in Units~7 and~8. The
mesh, the ghost cells at the two ends, the material models
(\cref{ch:materials}) and the steps that follow each cycle are the same as
method AV's.

\section{The equations as fluxes}
\label{sec:rs-fluxes}

Without a viscosity, $q = 0$ and $\sigma_{xx} = -P$. The Lagrangian
equations of mass, momentum and total energy,
\cref{eq:lag-mass,eq:lag-mom,eq:lag-total}, are then three conservation laws
in the mass coordinate $h$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{U}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}}{\partial h} = 0,
\qquad
\mathbf{U} = \begin{pmatrix} V \\ u \\ E \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} -u \\ P \\ P u \end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:rs-conservation}
\end{equation}
with $E = e + \tfrac{1}{2}u^2$ the total energy per unit mass. Each
component of $\mathbf{U}$ changes only through the difference of its flux
across a piece of material. In \cref{sec:balance} the stresses and the work
they do entered as sources; in $h$ they have become fluxes through the
faces.

Integrate \cref{eq:rs-conservation} over cell $k$, between $h_{k-1/2}$ and
$h_{k+1/2}$, whose mass is $M_k$, and over one step, from $t^n$ to
$t^{n+1} = t^n + \Delta t^n$:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{U}}^{n+1}_k = \bar{\mathbf{U}}^n_k
  - \frac{\Delta t^n}{M_k}\Bigl(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{k+1/2} - \hat{\mathbf{F}}_{k-1/2}\Bigr),
\label{eq:rs-finite-volume}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\mathbf{U}}_k$ is the cell's average over its mass and
$\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{k+1/2}$ is the flux through face $k + \tfrac{1}{2}$,
averaged over the step. Nothing has been approximated yet; this is the cell
balance of \cref{eq:cm-cell}, integrated over a step. Any method of this
form conserves mass, momentum and total energy, whatever its fluxes are, as
long as the two cells beside a face use the same flux there; in a sum over
the stack, the inner faces then cancel.

The design question is therefore how to estimate $\hat{\mathbf{F}}$, and
since $\mathbf{F} = (-u, P, Pu)$, a face's flux needs only two numbers, a
velocity and a pressure at that face. Method AV estimates them from the
cells' pressures, a viscosity, and a velocity carried on the face.
Godunov's answer is to solve, at each face, the problem the two cells
actually pose there: two constant states that meet at a face.

\section{Characteristics and acoustic invariants}
\label{sec:rs-characteristics}

For a smooth flow, the same equations can be written in the
\emph{primitive} variables $\mathbf{W} = (V, u, P)$. The pressure is a
function of the state, $P = P(V, e)$, and with $q = 0$ the first law is
$\dd e = -P\,\dd V$ (\cref{eq:lag-energy}). At a fixed $h$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
  = \Bigl(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\Bigr)_e \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
  + \Bigl(\frac{\partial P}{\partial e}\Bigr)_V \frac{\partial e}{\partial t}
  = -Z^2\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
  = -Z^2\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial h},
\label{eq:rs-pressure}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
Z^2 = P\Bigl(\frac{\partial P}{\partial e}\Bigr)_V
      - \Bigl(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\Bigr)_e
    = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\Bigr)_s
    = \rho^2 c^2 .
\label{eq:rs-z}
\end{equation}
The middle equality holds because $\dd e = -P\,\dd V$ is the isentrope, and
the last is the sound speed of \cref{eq:cm-c}. $Z = \rho c$ is the acoustic
impedance of \cref{eq:cm-impedance}, now taken at the local state rather
than the reference one. The three equations together are
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{W}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{A}\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{W}}{\partial h} = 0,
\qquad
\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & Z^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:rs-quasilinear}
\end{equation}
\trace{modules/rs_mod.f95}{rs_face_states}{ok_method_rs.deck}

\paragraph{Wave speeds.} The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ are the speeds at
which information moves through the material's mass, in mass per unit area
per unit time:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_1 = -Z, \qquad \lambda_2 = 0, \qquad \lambda_3 = +Z .
\label{eq:rs-eigen}
\end{equation}
The first is a left-running sound wave and the third a right-running one;
in distance, each moves at $c$ relative to the material. The second is the
material itself; in $h$ a material point never moves, so this wave is the
contact, which stays where it starts.

\paragraph{Invariants.} Multiply \cref{eq:rs-quasilinear} by a left
eigenvector $\boldsymbol{\ell}_i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ (one with
$\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\mathbf{A} = \lambda_i\boldsymbol{\ell}_i$). With $Z$
held fixed, the result is $\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\cdot\dd\mathbf{W} = 0$ along
the lines $\dd h/\dd t = \lambda_i$. The three eigenvectors are $(0, -Z,
1)$, $(Z^2, 0, 1)$ and $(0, Z, 1)$, so
\begin{equation}
\dd(P - Zu) = 0 \ \text{along}\ \frac{\dd h}{\dd t} = -Z,
\qquad
\dd P + Z^2\,\dd V = 0 \ \text{at fixed}\ h,
\qquad
\dd(P + Zu) = 0 \ \text{along}\ \frac{\dd h}{\dd t} = +Z .
\label{eq:rs-invariants}
\end{equation}
$P - Zu$ and $P + Zu$ are the acoustic system's \emph{Riemann invariants};
each is carried unchanged along its own family of characteristics. The
middle relation says that a material point's pressure and volume change
together along its isentrope. Across the contact, $V$ may jump by itself;
however, $u$ and $P$ may not, which is the contact condition of
\cref{eq:cm-contact}. A single right-running wave entering material at
rest carries $P + Zu$ from the wave's source and leaves $P - Zu = 0$ behind
it, so it carries $P = Zu$; this is \cref{sec:acoustics}'s result, now
derived from the invariants.

Holding $Z$ fixed is the \emph{acoustic approximation}. It is exact for a
wave so weak that it barely changes the material's state, and it is the
only approximation method RS makes in its Riemann problems.
\Cref{sec:rs-why-acoustic} says why it is enough even for strong shocks.

\section{The Riemann problem}
\label{sec:rs-riemann}

At $t = 0$, two constant states meet at a face, $\mathbf{W}_L$ on the left
and $\mathbf{W}_R$ on the right. This is the \emph{Riemann problem}. Three
waves leave the face, one for each eigenvalue (\cref{fig:rs-riemann}): a
left-running wave into the left state, the contact, which stays on the face
in $h$, and a right-running wave into the right state. Between the outer
two lies the \emph{star region}. There $u$ and $P$ take common values,
$u^*$ and $P^*$, on both sides of the contact, while $V$ takes two,
$V^*_L$ and $V^*_R$. In the full problem each outer wave is a shock or a
rarefaction fan, depending on the two states. In the acoustic
approximation each is a single line, at $-Z_L$ and $+Z_R$, with each
side's impedance held at its value in its initial state.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/riemann_schematic.pdf}
\caption{The Riemann problem: two constant states, three waves, and the
star region between the outer two. (a) The full problem in $t$--$x$, where
each outer wave may be a shock or a rarefaction fan, depending on the two
states. (b) The acoustic problem method RS solves, in $t$--$h$: the contact
stands still, and each outer wave is a single line, at $-Z_L$ and $+Z_R$.
Drawn from the course's Unit~7 sheet.}
\label{fig:rs-riemann}
\end{figure}

The solution follows from the invariants. A right-running characteristic,
$\dd h/\dd t = +Z_L$, crosses the left wave from the left state into the
star region, carrying $P + Z_L u$ with it. A left-running characteristic,
$\dd h/\dd t = -Z_R$, crosses the right wave from the right state, carrying
$P - Z_R u$. Both must hold in the star region:
\begin{equation}
P^* + Z_L u^* = P_L + Z_L u_L,
\qquad
P^* - Z_R u^* = P_R - Z_R u_R .
\label{eq:rs-lines}
\end{equation}
Each is a straight line in the $(u, P)$ plane, so this is the impedance
matching of \cref{sec:impedance-matching}, with each side's Hugoniot
replaced by the line through its state with slope $\mp Z$. The two lines
cross at
\begin{equation}
u^* = \frac{Z_L u_L + Z_R u_R + P_L - P_R}{Z_L + Z_R},
\qquad
P^* = \frac{Z_R P_L + Z_L P_R + Z_L Z_R\,(u_L - u_R)}{Z_L + Z_R}.
\label{eq:rs-star}
\end{equation}
\trace{modules/rs_mod.f95}{riemann_face}{ok_method_rs.deck, study_av.py}
These two expressions are the whole of method RS's Riemann solver; the
code evaluates them once per face per step, with $Z = \rho c$ from each
cell's average state at $t^n$.

\paragraph{Equal impedances.} With $Z_L = Z_R = Z$, \cref{eq:rs-star}
becomes
\begin{equation}
P^* = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(P_L + P_R\bigr) + \tfrac{1}{2}Z\,\bigl(u_L - u_R\bigr),
\qquad
u^* = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(u_L + u_R\bigr) + \frac{P_L - P_R}{2Z}.
\label{eq:rs-equal-z}
\end{equation}
The face pressure is the mean of the two cells' pressures, plus a term
proportional to how fast the two cells approach each other. That term is
a linear viscosity; it is method AV's linear term (\cref{eq:av-q}) with
$B_L = \tfrac{1}{2}$, built into the solution. Unlike AV's, however, it has
no switch, so it also lowers $P^*$ where the two cells move apart. It is
the dissipation method RS has in place of a $q$.

\paragraph{An impact.} A flyer of impedance $Z_L$ at velocity $u_0$ meets a
target of impedance $Z_R$ at rest, both at zero pressure. Then
\begin{equation}
u^* = \frac{Z_L}{Z_L + Z_R}\,u_0,
\qquad
P^* = \frac{Z_L Z_R}{Z_L + Z_R}\,u_0,
\label{eq:rs-impact}
\end{equation}
which is the acoustic impedance match, the crossing of the two dashed
lines in \cref{fig:pu-class}. For the class problem it gives 6.29~GPa at
the impact face on the first step; the exact first shock is 10.74~GPa.

\subsection{Why the acoustic solution is enough}
\label{sec:rs-why-acoustic}

On the class problem's first step, the acoustic solver is about 40\% low.
Yet method RS gets that shock's state right. The reason is the one of
\cref{sec:inside-front}: the Riemann solution supplies only the fluxes, and
the update, \cref{eq:rs-finite-volume}, conserves mass, momentum and
energy. Any shock that forms therefore carries the jump conditions of
\cref{ch:shocks} between its end states. Within a few steps, a strong shock
is spread over a few cells. Each face inside it then sees two neighbors
whose states differ by only a fraction of the whole jump, and for those
weak Riemann problems the acoustic solution is a good one. The acoustic
approximation shapes a shock's structure and its first few steps; it does
not set the shock's end states.
% SCOPE: an exact (nonlinear) Riemann solver, or a two-shock one. Update
% this paragraph if bitwhiz gains one (doc/manual/README.md, Scope).
A nonlinear Riemann solver would change the details within a few cells of a
shock, at the cost of an iteration at every face; \bw\ does not have one.

\section{Contacts and free surfaces}
\label{sec:rs-boundaries}

\paragraph{Contacts.} A material interface always lies on a face
(\cref{sec:mesh}), and needs no special treatment. The impedances on its
two sides differ, and \cref{eq:rs-star} already accounts for that; $u^*$
and $P^*$ are continuous across the contact, while each cell keeps its own
density and energy.

\paragraph{Free surfaces.} Beyond each end of the stack sits a ghost cell of
zero mass (\cref{fig:av-void}). Method RS gives it zero impedance, zero
pressure and zero velocity. At the left end, $Z_L = P_L = 0$ in
\cref{eq:rs-star}, and at the right end $Z_R = P_R = 0$:
\begin{equation}
u^*_{\mathrm{left}} = u_R - \frac{P_R}{Z_R},
\qquad
u^*_{\mathrm{right}} = u_L + \frac{P_L}{Z_L},
\qquad
P^* = 0 \ \text{at both ends}.
\label{eq:rs-free}
\end{equation}
\trace{modules/rs_mod.f95}{riemann_face}{ok_method_rs.deck, study_av.py}
The free-surface condition, \cref{eq:cm-free}, then holds exactly; the void
does no work and takes no momentum. For example, when a shock of particle
velocity $u_p$ reaches the right end, the end cell holds $u = u_p$ and, by
\cref{sec:rs-characteristics}, $P = Z u_p$. The end face then moves at
$2u_p$, which is the acoustic doubling of a free surface's velocity. For a
strong shock, the end states again come from conservation rather than from
the acoustic solution (\cref{sec:rs-measured}).

\paragraph{Zero impedance.} A void cell, stretched until its sound speed
vanishes (\cref{ch:materials}), has $Z = 0$ too. A face between a void cell
and a ghost, or between two void cells, then has $Z_L + Z_R = 0$, and
\cref{eq:rs-star} is zero over zero. There the code sets $P^* = 0$ and
$u^* = \tfrac{1}{2}(u_L + u_R)$, with a ghost taking its neighbor's
velocity (the RS sheet's boundary conditions). The class materials never
reach this case.

\section{Godunov's method, first order}
\label{sec:rs-first-order}

At first order (deck: \key{order = 1}), each cell's state is taken as
constant across the cell. The Riemann problem at face $k + \tfrac{1}{2}$
has the average of cell $k$ as its left state and the average of cell
$k + 1$ as its right state. No wave crosses a whole cell in one step
(\cref{sec:rs-dt}), so the waves from the neighboring faces never reach a
face within the step. Each face's star state therefore holds for the whole
step, and the step's average flux is exactly the star state's,
$\hat{\mathbf{F}} = (-u^*, P^*, P^*u^*)$. \Cref{eq:rs-finite-volume} then
gives the update:
\begin{gather}
x^{n+1}_{k+1/2} = x^n_{k+1/2} + \Delta t^n\,u^*_{k+1/2},
\qquad
\rho^{n+1}_k = \frac{M_k}{x^{n+1}_{k+1/2} - x^{n+1}_{k-1/2}},
\label{eq:rs-position}\\
u^{n+1}_k = u^n_k - \frac{\Delta t^n}{M_k}\Bigl(P^*_{k+1/2} - P^*_{k-1/2}\Bigr),
\label{eq:rs-momentum}\\
E^{n+1}_k = E^n_k - \frac{\Delta t^n}{M_k}\Bigl((P^*u^*)_{k+1/2} - (P^*u^*)_{k-1/2}\Bigr),
\qquad
e^{n+1}_k = E^{n+1}_k - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(u^{n+1}_k\bigr)^2 .
\label{eq:rs-energy}
\end{gather}
\trace{modules/rs_mod.f95}{rs_cell_step}{ok_method_rs_order1.deck, study_av.py}
The first line is the volume component of \cref{eq:rs-finite-volume}. A
cell's specific volume is its width over its mass, so moving each face by
$u^*$ and taking $\rho$ from the fixed mass gives $V^{n+1}_k = V^n_k +
(\Delta t^n/M_k)(u^*_{k+1/2} - u^*_{k-1/2})$ exactly. It also keeps the
faces on the material, which is what makes the mesh Lagrangian.

\paragraph{Conservation.} Each face's $P^*$ and $P^*u^*$ leave one cell and
enter the next. In a sum over the stack they cancel, except at the two ends,
where $P^* = 0$. The stack's mass, its momentum $\sum_k M_k u_k$ and its
total energy $\sum_k M_k E_k$ are therefore conserved to round-off. The
check script confirms it in exact rational arithmetic, on a small mesh with
a ghost at each end.

\paragraph{How it differs from method AV.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One place for each quantity.} The velocity is a cell average,
  not a face value, so there are no node masses. A face holds its position
  and its star state; $u^*$ is recomputed every step from the Riemann
  problem rather than carried from step to step.
\item \textbf{One time level.} Every cell quantity is at the whole steps
  $n$ and $n + 1$, and $u^*$ and $P^*$ belong to the step between them.
  There is no leapfrog, so there is no velocity step $\Delta t^{n-1/2}$
  either, and the first step needs no special start.
\item \textbf{No $q$.} The dissipation comes from the Riemann solution,
  \cref{eq:rs-equal-z}, and from replacing each cell's state by its average
  every step.
\item \textbf{Energy.} The conserved variable is the total energy $E$, and
  the internal energy is what remains after the cell's kinetic energy.
  Method AV also conserves total energy, but it charges each cell
  differently (\cref{sec:av-energy}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What first order costs.} First order smears a shock. On the
aluminum square-shock deck (\code{tests/verification/av_study/fs_square.deck};
$u_p$ from 0.5 to 2~km/s, 25 and 100 cells per mm, measured 1.5~mm into the
target), a shock's velocity rises from 10\% to 90\% of its jump across 3.7
to 8.8 cells, and the weaker the shock, the wider it is
(\code{checks/ch5_rs.py}). The built-in viscosity is linear in the velocity
jump. A shock's width is set by the balance between its viscosity and its
own steepening; the steepening is weak for a weak shock, so a linear
viscosity spreads it widely. Method AV's quadratic term grows with the jump
and keeps its widths nearly independent of strength (2.1 to 3.2 cells,
\cref{sec:av-q}).

\section{Second order: MUSCL--Hancock}
\label{sec:rs-second-order}

At second order (deck: \key{order = 2}, the default), method RS keeps the
update and the Riemann solver, and changes only the states each Riemann
problem is given. Two steps produce them.
\trace{modules/rs_mod.f95}{rs_face_states, vanleer}{ok_method_rs.deck, compare_hvrb.py}

\paragraph{Slopes.} Within each cell, $u$ and $P$ are taken to vary
linearly in $h$, with slopes $s_u$ and $s_P$ per unit mass. For each of
them, call it $W$, the one-sided slopes to the two neighbors are taken over
the mass between the cell centers,
\begin{equation}
a = \frac{W_k - W_{k-1}}{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(M_{k-1} + M_k\bigr)},
\qquad
b = \frac{W_{k+1} - W_k}{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(M_k + M_{k+1}\bigr)},
\label{eq:rs-onesided}
\end{equation}
so a mesh whose zone size changes at an interface is handled. The slope used
is Van Leer's harmonic mean of the two,
\begin{equation}
s = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{2ab}{a + b} & \text{if } ab > 0, \\[1ex]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rs-vanleer}
\end{equation}
This limited slope lies between $a$ and $b$, and never exceeds twice the
smaller of the two. On a mesh of equal masses, each of the line's values at
a face therefore lies between the averages of the two cells beside that
face. At a peak or a trough,
$ab \le 0$ and the slope is zero (\cref{fig:rs-muscl}). The reconstruction
thus makes no new extremum, and it does not ring at a shock the way an
unlimited second-order scheme does.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/rs_muscl.pdf}
\caption{The second-order reconstruction of $u$ or $P$ over five cells of
equal mass. Dashed: the cell averages. Solid: the lines with Van Leer's
slopes, and their values at the faces. A peak, and a cell beside a ghost,
get no slope.}
\label{fig:rs-muscl}
\end{figure}

Only $u$ and $P$ are reconstructed. Both are continuous across a material
interface, so a slope may span one. The specific volume jumps at an
interface, so it is never reconstructed; instead, each cell's $Z$ comes from
its average state. A cell beside a
ghost gets zero slopes, so the two end cells, and with them the free
surfaces, stay first order.

\paragraph{The half step.} The values at a cell's two faces, $W_k \mp
\tfrac{1}{2}M_k s$, are then advanced half a step with the cell's own
acoustic equations, \cref{eq:rs-quasilinear}, with the slopes in place of
the derivatives:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_{l,r} &= u_k \mp \tfrac{1}{2}M_k s_u - \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t^n\, s_P, \\
P_{l,r} &= P_k \mp \tfrac{1}{2}M_k s_P - \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t^n Z_k^2\, s_u ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rs-halfstep}
\end{equation}
with $l$ taking the upper sign and $r$ the lower. This is Hancock's
predictor. The face states are now centered at $n + \tfrac{1}{2}$, the
middle of the step, so the fluxes are centered in time as well as in space.
A cell's left-face values become the right state of the Riemann problem on
face $k - \tfrac{1}{2}$, and its right-face values the left state on face $k
+ \tfrac{1}{2}$. The predictor itself is not conservative, since it uses the
primitive equations. However, it only shapes the states the Riemann
problems are given; the update is still
\cref{eq:rs-position,eq:rs-momentum,eq:rs-energy}, so mass, momentum and
total energy are conserved exactly as at first order.

A cell that burns during the step adds its reaction's source to the
pressure's half step, $+\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t^n\rho c^2\sigma r$, held over
the step as $Z$ is (\cref{sec:rs-source}). Order 1 has no predictor, and so
no source.

\paragraph{What second order buys.} On the same square-shock deck, a
shock's 10--90\% rise spans 1.5 to 2.6 cells. That is narrower than at
first order in every case, and narrower than method AV's 2.1 to 3.2 cells
with its default $B_Q$. The order of accuracy on smooth flow, second order by
design, has not yet been measured in a test (\code{TODO.md}, section~8).

\section{The time step}
\label{sec:rs-dt}

In $h$, waves leave each face at $-Z$ and $+Z$, and cell $k$ is $M_k$ of
mass across. A wave therefore crosses it in $M_k/Z_k$, which in distance is
its width over its sound speed:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t_k = \frac{x^{n+1}_{k+1/2} - x^{n+1}_{k-1/2}}{c^{n+1}_k}
           = \frac{M_k}{Z^{n+1}_k},
\qquad
\Delta t^{n+1} = \mathrm{CFL}\,\min_k \Delta t_k ,
\label{eq:rs-cfl}
\end{equation}
\trace{modules/rs_mod.f95}{rs_cell_dt, rs_state}{ok_method_rs.deck}
with CFL the same deck key as for method AV (\key{CFL}, 0.6 by default).
The flow speed $|u|$ does not appear. The mesh moves with the material, so
the flow carries no wave across a cell; only sound does. Method AV adds
$|\bar u|$ to its limit (\cref{eq:av-cfl}) as a margin, so for the same
state method RS's step is never shorter. A void cell, whose sound speed is
zero, is limited by its own speed instead, $\Delta x/|u|$, and a void cell at
rest sets no limit.

\section{One cycle}
\label{sec:rs-cycle}

\bw\ runs a cycle of method RS as one routine of four loops over the mesh (three without a burn)
(\code{rs_region} in \code{maincode.f95}). Each loop finishes before the
next begins:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[0$'$.] \textbf{Burn rate:} only when the deck has a burn. Each cell
  that can react takes its burn model's step from its pressure at $n$, and
  the reaction's source for the predictor (\code{burn_rate_rs},
  \cref{sec:rs-source}).
\item[0.] \textbf{Face states:} each cell sets the $u$ and $P$ it offers
  the Riemann problems on its two faces. At order 1 these are the cell's own
  averages; at order 2 they are the reconstructed values after the half
  step, \cref{eq:rs-halfstep}.
\item[1.] \textbf{Riemann problems:} each face takes its $u^*$ and $P^*$,
  \cref{eq:rs-star}, and moves to $x^{n+1}$, \cref{eq:rs-position}.
\item[2.] \textbf{Cell update:} each cell takes its new density, velocity
  and internal energy, \cref{eq:rs-position,eq:rs-momentum,eq:rs-energy}.
  A cell that can burn also carries its solid's isentrope energy $e_S$ to
  the new volume, and updates where its solid is held in tension
  (\cref{sec:tension}). Finally, every cell takes its pressure, with the
  spall rule, its sound speed and its time step limit $\Delta t_k$,
  \cref{eq:rs-cfl}; a cell that has begun to burn takes its pressure and
  sound speed from the closure of \cref{ch:burning-cell}
  (\cref{sec:one-cycle}).
\end{enumerate}
\trace{main/maincode.f95}{rs_region}{ok_method_rs.deck, compare_hvrb.py}

After the loops, a cycle continues exactly as method AV's does
(\cref{sec:av-cycle}): the clock advances, the next step is set, each cell
that carries a temperature updates it, and the lead-shock detector looks at
the new state. As for method AV, each loop reads only what the loop before
it wrote, so the loops run in parallel with OpenMP and give the serial
answer bit for bit.

\paragraph{What the outputs hold.} The velocity in a tracer file
(\key{KMPS}) and in the history frames is the velocity the method carries
at a face. For method RS that is $u^*$, the velocity of the step just
taken. Method RS has no $q$, so a tracer file's \key{Q} column holds NaN.

\section{What method RS does, measured}
\label{sec:rs-measured}

Against exact answers and method AV, in \code{doc/design/av_study.md}
(\code{tests/verification/study_av.py}) and \code{checks/ch5_rs.py}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Shock width.} At order 2, 1.5 to 2.6 cells from 10\% to 90\%
  of the velocity jump; at order 1, 3.7 to 8.8 cells, wider for weaker
  shocks (aluminum, $u_p$ from 0.5 to 2~km/s, 25 and 100 cells per mm).
\item \textbf{At a free surface,} order 2 overshoots the exact velocity by
  0.37\% to 0.39\% when a shock arrives, on every mesh from 25 to 1600 cells
  per mm, and its plateau converges to the exact value. Method AV
  overshoots by about 6\% (\cref{sec:av-measured}).
\item \textbf{At a detonation,} order 2's front speed is within 0.02\% of
  $D_{\mathrm{CJ}}$ on every mesh from 25 cells per mm, where method AV is
  3\% fast.
\item \textbf{Conservation.} Mass, momentum and total energy are conserved
  to round-off at both orders.
\end{itemize}

Method AV remains the class project's method. Method RS at order 2 is the
better choice where the measurement is a free surface's velocity, or a
detonation front's speed on a coarse mesh.
